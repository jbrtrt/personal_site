01 — About

How I got here.

I was accepted to a US medical school at sixteen and turned it down. Four years of engineering first looked like the better way to be useful in a hospital later, and I spent the years before college at benches that made the case. At Regeneron, in Genome Engineering Technologies, I expressed and purified genetic variants of a nociceptive-specific sodium channel — the protein that decides whether a nerve fires at all — so they could be screened against drugs and used to model pain. At the Fricker Lab at Albert Einstein I designed original assays for how neuropeptides change the shape of striatal cells, and did the quantitative peptidomics behind them: mass spectrometry read as data rather than as pictures. That was the first time a membrane current was something I had to make work.

Columbia followed — a B.S. in biomedical engineering with a computer science minor, the best senior thesis in the department, a co-founded Systems Biology Initiative and the only undergraduate journal club on campus. In 2017 I was one of eight students sent to the iGEM Jamboree, where we engineered probiotic bacteria to invade tumour cells and deliver shRNA from the inside. In the Artificial Organs Lab, under the people who built that field, I worked on an ambulatory hemodialysis and filtration rig and on the blood-volume control problem that decides whether dialysis can ever leave the clinic. That problem is still the one I find hardest to put down.

Then four years as founder and CEO of ViveSense, building core hardware for a patented at-home male fertility test: an NSF SBIR Phase I application, the NSF I-Corps program, and forty-plus hours of interviews with physicians and with the men nobody asks. Four years is long enough to find out which of your assumptions were load-bearing. Two patents went in before I was twenty-three and have since been granted. I am at Tufts now for an M.D., after a master’s on hydrocele and its treatment, and I have had an EMT patch on since 2019 — 911 across five boroughs, and on call at Madison Square Garden, Yankee Stadium and the US Open.

I run tests before I trust a result, in the lab or in a repo.

02 — Phase lock

The gap between what exists in healthcare and what is possible in engineering is massive. Most builders never close it, because they’ve never been on the other side of the exam table.

I went to medical school to close it myself instead of guessing at it.

  3. M3 · stoneidx

    A modest number you can defend beats a good one you cannot.

    A comorbidity index for kidney stones

    Five independent arms fit across national discharge data, then harmonised into one integer point table. Firth penalised regression where the data separates, tetrachoric correlation written from scratch, factor count chosen by parallel analysis rather than an eigenvalue rule of thumb. The unit of analysis is the encounter, never the person — enforced by a lint test that fails the build if a source file says otherwise.

    Status
    Five arms fit, harmonised to one point table
    Data
    HCUP NIS / NEDS / NASS / NRD · 4.1M encounters
    Result
    Pooled AUC 0.713 across 19 cohorts
    Method
    Firth penalised regression · cluster-robust · Python

    Modest AUC is the expected result. Nothing here is tuned toward a number.

  5. M5 · FlopCheck

    The limitation is the interesting part.

    Did the contact actually do that?

    Three-dimensional pose off broadcast video, then an impulse-momentum check against the motion that followed: was the reaction consistent with the contact, or embellished? Built out of my own impact-detection patent. The honest part is the limitation — monocular depth, not the pose model, is the accuracy ceiling, and the usual benchmarks are root-relative, so they hide exactly the quantity the physics consumes.

    Status
    Research build
    Method
    3D pose off broadcast video · inverse dynamics · Monte Carlo envelope
    Patent
    Extends US12511900B2
    Ceiling

  6. M6 · Lantern

    When it matters most, the right move is to say less.

    A steady presence for the hours between visits

    A bedside companion for people living with serious illness, for the long quiet stretches when no one is in the room. The safety layer is deterministic and runs on every utterance independently of the model; inferred memory is never voiced as fact; prognosis, diagnosis and dosing return refusals rather than answers; and a human is one touch away from every screen. It is the only thing I have built where the correct behaviour is usually to say less.

  7. M7 · notes2anki

    Nothing ships because it reads better. It ships because it won.

    Flashcards that grade themselves, then argue back

    Medical notes carry meaning in their formatting — highlight colour, indentation, an arrow, an odd capitalisation. The pipeline reads that structure, writes cards against a real study standard, then judges them on whether they actually test understanding. Weak cards get one rewrite and are otherwise dropped. The prompts that produce them are revised and adopted only after a blind A/B gate says the new version is better.
