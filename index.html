01 — About

How I got here.

I was accepted to a US medical school at sixteen and turned it down. I chose engineering instead because I wanted to understand biological problems at the level where they could be measured, modeled, and rebuilt. That decision took me from engineering modified pain-related ion channels at Regeneron and designing neuropeptide assays at Albert Einstein to studying biomedical engineering and computer science at Columbia. There, I wrote the department’s top senior thesis, co-founded the Systems Biology Initiative, created the university’s only undergraduate journal club, and joined an iGEM team that engineered probiotic bacteria that killed cancer.

Engineering showed me what could be built. Patients showed me what was worth building. In the Artificial Organs Lab, I worked on ambulatory dialysis and the blood-volume control problem that keeps renal replacement therapy tied to the clinic — something I’d seen over years as an EMT. I later founded ViveSense and spent four years developing patented hardware for at-home male fertility testing. More than forty hours of interviews with physicians and the men navigating infertility changed how I understood invention: the technical system matters, but so do the room it enters, the person who must use it, and the assumptions its builders never thought to question.

I chose medicine not as a departure from engineering, but as a way to choose better problems and build more useful solutions. Alongside medical training at Tufts, I have continued developing medical devices and intelligent healthcare systems. My goal is to build medical devices and intelligent healthcare systems that make diagnostics more accessible, monitoring more continuous between visits, and clinical work more informed. The future I want to help build does not ask patients or clinicians to adapt themselves to technology. It asks technology to understand them first.

I run tests before I trust a result, in the lab or in a repo.

02 — Phase lock

The gap between what exists in healthcare and what is possible in engineering is massive. Most builders never close it, because they’ve never been on the other side of the exam table.

I went to medical school to close it myself instead of guessing at it.

Many of healthcare’s most important unmet needs are difficult to see from outside the clinic. Working across medicine and engineering lets me identify those problems firsthand, translate them into technical requirements, and build systems around how care actually happens. The projects below are the result.
